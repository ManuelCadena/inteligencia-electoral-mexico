0.1Research question

Can a multimodal system — combining historical vote series, macroeconomic and territorial covariates, geographic neighborhood structure and political discourse text — produce subnational electoral forecasts in Mexico that are better calibrated than a naive persistence baseline, while explaining which channel contributes incremental signal?

Operational sub-questions:

  1. SQ1 (tabular): does a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) over structural plus exogenous features beat multinomial logistic regression and persistence on vote share and winner? P
  2. SQ2 (temporal): do recurrent networks — Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) — capture inter-cycle dependence (sexenio punishment/reward) that an MLP misses? P
  3. SQ3 (spatial): does message passing in a Graph Neural Network (GNN) over municipal adjacency reduce subnational error versus aspatial models? P
  4. SQ4 (textual): do features derived from presidential discourse (topic shares, stance, territorial attention via Named Entity Recognition) carry incremental predictive power? P
  5. SQ5 (latent): does a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) over electoral profiles recover the regional blocs documented in the literature (industrial north, ancestral south, urban center)? P

Part I

The Mexican electoral system — institutional context for interpreting the data

Without this part, SHARE_TOTAL, GEO_KEY_2 or a coalition string like PAN_PRI_PRD are just tokens. This section translates the legal-territorial architecture into executable analysis rules. Base source: docs/KB_SISTEMA_ELECTORAL_MEXICO.md. CPEUMINE

I.1Institutions and official sources

Mexico operates a dual electoral federalism. The Instituto Nacional Electoral (INE) — the national electoral management body created in its current form by the 2014 reform that succeeded the Instituto Federal Electoral (IFE) — organizes federal contests: President, Federal Deputies and Senators. The 32 Organismos Públicos Locales Electorales (OPLE) — one local electoral institute per state — organize gubernatorial, local-deputy and municipal races. The Tribunal Electoral del Poder Judicial de la Federación (TEPJF) and its regional chambers adjudicate challenges — which matters analytically because annulled elections (e.g., several Chiapas municipalities in 2015) appear in the source with zero votes and must not be confused with abstention. K

Three result sources with distinct legal status: PREP (Programa de Resultados Electorales Preliminares — preliminary, unofficial, election-night count), the distrital computations (legally official tallies), and SICEE (Sistema de Consulta de la Estadística de las Elecciones — INE's consolidated historical statistics). Our dataset is built from SICEE: definitive, but containing source defects the pipeline flags with FLAG_CONSISTENCIA rather than silently correcting. KD

I.2Electoral geography — the territorial nesting chain

The atomic unit is the electoral section (~68–71 thousand nationwide, each covering 100–3,000 voters), which contains polling stations (casillas, ~170 thousand in 2024). Sections aggregate upward into local single-member districts (699 across all 32 states), federal single-member districts (300, per articles 52–53 of the Constitución Política de los Estados Unidos Mexicanos, CPEUM), municipalities (~2,469 plus 16 Mexico City alcaldías), and entities (32 states). For the Senate no federal district exists: senators are elected at the entity level. K

I.3Seat-allocation formulas — why vote share is not seat share

Two principles coexist. Mayoría Relativa (MR) — single-member plurality: whoever gets the most votes in the district wins the seat. Representación Proporcional (RP) — proportional representation: seats allocated from party lists by quotient and largest-remainder formulas, subject to constitutional caps (no party may exceed 300 federal deputies; an 8-point overrepresentation cap limits the gap between national vote share and seat share). The Senate adds a third device: primera minoría (first minority) — one seat per entity for the runner-up slate. K

I.4Parties and coalitions — the most fragile semantic field

PARTIDO_COALICION takes values like PAN, MORENA or PAN_PRI_PRD. The acronyms: PAN = Partido Acción Nacional (center-right, founded 1939); PRI = Partido Revolucionario Institucional (the hegemonic party 1929–2000); PRD = Partido de la Revolución Democrática (left, 1989); MORENA = Movimiento Regeneración Nacional (registered 2014); PT = Partido del Trabajo; PVEM = Partido Verde Ecologista; MC = Movimiento Ciudadano. A coalition is a joint candidacy, not a party: summing PAN + PRI + PAN_PRI_PRD rows in the same election can double-count when the source reports the coalition and its components separately. K Decision taken (Sprint 1): each coalition string is treated as a single actor exactly as reported by the source — what the voter saw on the ballot — with a mandatory sensitivity run redistributing coalition votes to component parties (documented in docs/DECISION_COALICIONES.md). The canonical dataset preserves source values; the consistency flag marks groups whose share sums fall outside [0.9, 1.1]. D

Critical acronym chronology: recent party extinctions — PANAL (Partido Nueva Alianza), PES (Partido Encuentro Social), RSP (Redes Sociales Progresistas), FXM (Fuerza por México), all de-registered after 2021 — and the rise of MORENA. A longitudinal model cannot treat the party vocabulary as stationary: it needs an equivalence table or per-cycle learned embeddings (see IV.5). K

I.5Historical electoral map 1988–2024

  • PRI hegemony (–1988): party-state regime; effectively competitive elections begin with the 1988 cardenista rupture. Pre-1991 SICEE data show low real competitiveness — useful as a baseline, dangerous as a signal. K
  • Transition (1988–2000): alternation in Baja California 1989 (first opposition governor), presidential alternation 2000 (PAN). K
  • North–south polarization (2006–2012): PAN strong in the industrial north; PRD in Mexico City and the south; PRI territorially resilient. KL
  • MORENA nationalization (2018–2024): historically unprecedented territorial sweep — in 2018 MORENA wins entities that had never voted left; 2024 widens the map. For modeling: a regime change that invalidates pre-2018 regularities unless temporal weighting or per-regime parameters are used. K

I.7Temporal-comparability warnings

Non-comparability checklist
  • Federal redistricting 2005/2017/2022: federal ID_DISTRITO is not stable across processes — a panel analysis by district that crosses a redistricting must bridge via section or municipality, or treat the district as a new unit. K
  • DF→CDMX (2016): the pipeline maps Distrito Federal to CIUDAD DE MEXICO; alcaldías comparable to municipalities from 2018. D
  • Usos y costumbres: ~418 of 570 Oaxaca municipalities select authorities without partisan elections — producing structural zeros, not abstention. Model separately or exclude. K
  • Re-election: municipal re-election exists only from ~2018; federal legislative re-election from 2021 — incumbency variables are censored by nature, not by missing data. K
  • Staggered gubernatorial calendars: state cycles do not coincide; in 2015–2024 each entity has its own calendar — ANIO alone does not define the panel; the unit is (office × entity). K

III.1 The Multi-Layer Perceptron — the honest tabular baseline

Neural-network fundamentals: units, activation functions, forward and backward propagation.

Why it exists

An election is, to first approximation, a nonlinear function of tabular features: vote history, marginalization, incumbency, macroeconomics. Before any exotic architecture we need the baseline answering: how much signal lives in the table alone, with no explicit structure?

Theoretical framework

A neuron computes an affine map plus a nonlinearity: y = σ(w·x + b). The Universal Approximation Theorem (Cybenko 1989; Hornik 1991) states that a single hidden layer with enough units approximates any continuous function on a compact set — the existence guarantee; depth is the efficiency story (functions computable with exponentially fewer units deep than wide; Eldan & Shamir 2016). L

Activation functions, expanded: ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) = max(0, z) — cheap, non-saturating, but can "die" at 0; GELU (Gaussian Error Linear Unit) = x·Φ(x) where Φ is the standard-normal CDF — a smooth probabilistic gating used by Transformers; softmax turns a real vector z ∈ R^K into a probability vector exp(z_i)/Σ_j exp(z_j) lying in the (K−1)-simplex.

h[l] = σ( W[l] h[l−1] + b[l] ),  ŷ = softmax( W[L] h[L−1] + b[L] )

Loss: categorical cross-entropy CE = −Σ_k y_k log ŷ_k measures the divergence between predicted and empirical party-share distributions; it is the MLE objective for a categorical model and the natural metric for our compositional outcome (shares sum to 1). Backpropagation is the chain rule applied layer-wise — computing ∂L/∂W^[l] by propagating δ^[l] = (∂L/∂h^[l]) ⊙ σ′ — enabling gradient descent in O(#params) memory. L

III.2 PyTorch — the reusable experiment harness

Tensors, autograd, Dataset/DataLoader, optimizers, devices.

Why it exists

The pedagogical value is the pipeline, not the framework: a Dataset that materializes the canonical key, a DataLoader yielding election-geography-consistent batches, and a training loop logging metrics per cohort (year × level). A defective harness contaminates every downstream experiment.

Theoretical framework

PyTorch is an imperative autodiff engine: operations on tensors record a computational graph (tape), and loss.backward() traverses it applying the chain rule (reverse-mode automatic differentiation). The optimizer updates parameters by a descent rule — SGD (Stochastic Gradient Descent) θ ← θ − η∇L, or Adam (Adaptive Moments) which keeps per-parameter exponential moving averages of gradient mean m and variance v with bias correction, adapting the step size: θ ← θ − η·m̂/(√v̂+ε). L

θt+1 = θt − η·∇θL(θt; Bt),    ∇θ via chain rule (autograd)

III.3 Regularization and AI-assisted development

Dropout, weight decay, early stopping, data augmentation; AI-assisted coding practice.

Why it exists

With a small effective n — elections are rare events: ~200 presidential entity-year groups in the whole panel — regularization is the model. Week 3 translates to: how far can an MLP go without overfitting, and how does AI assistance accelerate development without compromising integrity.

Theoretical framework

Bias–variance tradeoff: expected test error decomposes into bias² + variance + irreducible noise; capacity raises variance. Regularization injects a prior penalty on complexity. L2 / weight decay adds λ‖W‖² to the loss — equivalent (for SGD) to a Gaussian prior on weights, shrinking them toward 0. Dropout (Srivastava et al. 2014) masks each hidden unit with probability p at train time and rescales at test — an ensemble of ~2^n thinned subnetworks sharing weights, discouraging co-adaptation. Early stopping halts when validation error stops improving — a form of implicit capacity control. L

Lreg = LCE + λ‖W‖² ,   Dropout: h̃ = h ⊙ Bernoulli(p) / p

Early stopping is computed on the temporal validation slice (2015–2018), never a random split: it halts when out-of-time generalization stops improving — the only honest notion of generalization for electoral series. P

III.4 Convolutional Neural Networks — spatial texture in the vote

Why it exists

Theoretical framework

III.6 Autoencoders and VAEs — the electoral map as a latent manifold

Why it exists

Mexico's electoral map is approximable by few latent dimensions — "industrial north", "ancestral south", "urban center", "border". An Autoencoder (AE) forces a municipality's full electoral profile through a 2–3-dimensional bottleneck; if the manifold exists, emergent clusters should match (or refute) the literature's typology. The Variational Autoencoder (VAE, Kingma & Welling 2013) adds probabilistic structure: soft latent regions instead of hard clusters.

Theoretical framework

AE: encoder z = f(x), decoder x̂ = g(z); trained to minimize reconstruction error ‖x−x̂‖², the bottleneck forces a compressed code z capturing the dominant factors of variation. Manifold hypothesis: high-dimensional data lie near a low-dimensional manifold; the AE is a nonlinear generalization of PCA (Principal Component Analysis) that learns it. VAE: the encoder outputs a posterior q(z|x); training maximizes the ELBO (Evidence Lower Bound) — reconstruction term minus the KL divergence (Kullback–Leibler) pulling q(z|x) toward a standard-normal prior. The reparameterization trick z = μ + σ·ε, ε~N(0,I) keeps the sampling differentiable. L

ELBO = Eq(z|x)[log p(x|z)] − KL( q(z|x) ‖ N(0,I) ) x = municipality-year share vector; z = latent electoral profile

Dataset binding

III.7 Transformers — attention over electoral history

Self-attention, positional encoding, encoder/decoder, context without recurrence.

Why it exists

A recurrent network must "pass through" 2006 to influence 2024; attention accesses any past cycle directly. For one entity, the question "how much does the 2018 anomaly weigh versus the 1991–2017 regime?" is literally an attention distribution — the mechanism is simultaneously a predictor and an interpretability instrument.

Theoretical framework

Scaled dot-product self-attention (Vaswani et al. 2017): each position emits a query Q, key K and value V; the output is a weighted sum of values where weights measure query–key compatibility, scaled by √d_k to keep softmax gradients stable. Multi-head attention (MHA) runs h parallel attention functions on different projections — different heads can attend to long regime, recent shock and sexenio cycle simultaneously. Positional encoding injects order (sinusoidal or learned); with irregular local calendars we use a continuous ANIO embedding. Causal masking blocks attention to future positions — attending to the future is leakage by definition. L

Attn(Q,K,V) = softmax( QK⊤ / √dk ) · V Q,K,V = projections of the unit's electoral-cycle sequence

A Transformer block stacks: LayerNorm (normalizes activations per position) → MHA → residual connection → position-wise feed-forward (two dense layers, GELU) → residual. Complexity O(T²·d) in sequence length — the reason state-space alternatives exist (III.14). L

III.9 Seq2Seq and LSTM/GRU — the memory of the electoral cycle

Encoder-decoder, LSTM/GRU gates, teacher forcing, sequence forecasting.

Why it exists

The vote has structural memory: incumbent punishment, stronghold inertia, the sexenio effect. A recurrent network over each unit's cycle sequence is the honest neural temporal baseline — expressive enough for the dynamics, small enough for the available n.

Theoretical framework

An RNN (Recurrent Neural Network) maintains hidden state h_t = f(h_{t−1}, x_t); plain RNNs suffer vanishing gradients over long spans. LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory, Hochreiter & Schmidhuber 1997) adds a cell state c_t and three gates — input i, forget f, output o — that learn what to write, keep and emit: c_t = f⊙c_{t−1} + i⊙tanh(c̃). GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit, Cho et al. 2014) merges the gates (update z, reset r) for fewer parameters. Seq2seq (encoder–decoder) maps an input sequence to a context vector h_T, then decodes the target autoregressively; teacher forcing feeds the true previous output during training. L

ct = ft⊙ct−1 + it⊙c̃t , ht = ot⊙tanh(ct) the forget gate decides what the system "remembers" — regime vs anomaly

Theoretical framework

CRF (Lafferty et al. 2001): a discriminative undirected graphical model — P(y|x) ∝ exp(Σ_t [ s(y_t,x,t) + A_{y_{t−1},y_t} ]) — where s scores emission and A is the learned transition matrix; decoding by Viterbi. BERT: a bidirectional Transformer encoder pre-trained with Masked Language Modeling (predict masked tokens) and next-sentence objectives; fine-tuning adapts it to a task with a small head. L

Card 1

Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) — tabular baseline

Selection rationale. An election, to first approximation, is a nonlinear function of a fixed vector of covariates: past shares, macroeconomics, marginalization, incumbency, and structural dummies. When there is no explicit ordering in the input, the simplest universal approximator is the honest baseline. The MLP's only inductive bias is smoothness and compositionality of functions; it does not invent spatial or temporal structure that may not exist.

What it does. An MLP maps an input vector x ∈ R^F through one or more hidden layers h = σ(Wx+b) to a target ŷ. With a softmax output it produces a probability distribution over parties; with a linear output it predicts a scalar such as turnout. The Universal Approximation Theorem (Cybenko 1989; Hornik 1991) guarantees that a wide enough single-hidden-layer network can approximate any continuous function on a compact set, while depth makes that approximation efficient (Eldan & Shamir 2016).

Core concepts, expanded. A neuron computes an affine transformation w·x + b followed by a nonlinearity σ. ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) returns max(0,z); it is computationally cheap but can "die" when the input is always negative. GELU (Gaussian Error Linear Unit) returns z·Φ(z), where Φ is the CDF (Cumulative Distribution Function) of the standard normal distribution — a smooth probabilistic gating used in Transformers. Softmax converts a real vector into a probability vector by exponentiating and normalizing. Cross-Entropy (CE) measures the disagreement between the predicted distribution and the true one; it is the Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) loss for a categorical model. Backpropagation applies the chain rule of calculus layer by layer to compute how each weight affects the loss, so gradient descent can update it.

Inputs. One row per election-geography unit: lagged party shares, lagged macroeconomic and remittance features, structural dummies, and learned embeddings for entity, party and office.

Outputs. Party-share vector (softmax, sums to 1), scalar turnout, and a winner class.

Training procedure. Minimize CE plus L2 weight decay using SGD (Stochastic Gradient Descent) or Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation, which keeps exponential moving averages of gradient mean and variance and rescales the learning rate per parameter). Early stopping is monitored on the temporal validation slice (2015–2018), never on the test set. The scaler is fit only on the training split to avoid leakage.

Evaluation. Mean Absolute Error (MAE) on party shares, top-1 winner accuracy, Brier score (probabilistic calibration of the winner), and Expected Calibration Error (ECE). The mandatory comparison is against a persistence baseline ("predict the previous election's shares").

Electoral interpretation. The MLP answers the research question: how much signal lives in the table alone, with no explicit time or space model? If the MLP cannot beat persistence, more complex models must justify every additional parameter.

Assumptions. (i) Conditional on the engineered features, observations are exchangeable — the model has no memory and no geography. (ii) The target shares lie in a probability simplex. (iii) Past votes and macro variables contain enough information to forecast the next election.

Limitations. It cannot exploit sequence, spatial adjacency, or text. It is vulnerable to overfitting when n is small (only ~6–8 presidential cycles per unit), so regularization is essential. Coalition labels such as PAN_PRI_PRD are treated as atomic tokens unless a separate redistribution sensitivity is run.

Card 2

Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) — spatial texture

Selection rationale. The vote has local spatial texture: northern manufacturing belts, southern indigenous strongholds, urban corridors. A CNN learns local pattern detectors with shared parameters, so the same kernel that detects "opposition island" in one region applies everywhere. It is appropriate when the input can be arranged as a grid or a sequence with local neighborhoods.

What it does. A convolution slides a small kernel across the input and computes local dot products. Weight sharing means the same kernel is applied at every position; translation equivariance means a pattern is detected wherever it appears. Stacking layers grows the receptive field, so deep units see regional context. Pooling (max or mean) makes the representation locally invariant and reduces resolution.

Inputs. Two options are declared: (a) a year × party × entity tensor for federal-level analysis; (b) a true geographic grid of municipalities (requires INEGI geometry or a hexbin raster). Option (a) is a correlation detector, not a spatial one; option (b) is the faithful spatial operator.

Outputs. Learned feature maps, followed by a dense head for shares or winner.

Training procedure. Cross-entropy on party shares, with dropout after the global-pooling layer. The kernel size is chosen so the receptive field covers a few neighboring units; padding preserves border information.

Evaluation. MAE and winner accuracy versus the MLP baseline on the identical split; qualitative inspection of the first-layer kernels as spatial filters.

Electoral interpretation. The CNN tests whether local geographic texture adds predictive signal beyond tabular features. If it does not, geography may be better captured by an explicit graph (GNN) or by entity embeddings.

Assumptions. (i) Nearby positions in the input are semantically related (Tobler's first law). (ii) The input grid is topologically meaningful; a year×party arrangement is not a true map and must not be reported as spatial.

Limitations. Edge effects make Baja California and Yucatán boundaries special. Irregular municipal borders do not fit a rectangular grid; the CNN is therefore a precursor to the GNN, not its substitute.

Card 3

Autoencoder (AE) / Variational Autoencoder (VAE) — latent electoral profiles

Selection rationale. There are no official labels for "electoral regions" of Mexico beyond coarse geographic names. An unsupervised bottleneck learns a low-dimensional representation of a municipality's full voting profile, revealing clusters that may match — or refute — the literature's typology (north, south, center, border). The VAE adds a probabilistic latent space suitable for simulation and anomaly detection.

What it does. An AE (Autoencoder) has an encoder z = f(x) and a decoder x̂ = g(z); it minimizes reconstruction error ‖x − x̂‖². The bottleneck forces a compressed code. A VAE (Variational Autoencoder) makes the encoder output a distribution q(z|x) instead of a single point; training maximizes the ELBO (Evidence Lower Bound), which is the expected reconstruction log-likelihood minus the KL divergence (Kullback–Leibler divergence) from the posterior to a standard-normal prior. The reparameterization trick z = μ + σ·ε, with ε ~ N(0,I), keeps sampling differentiable.

Inputs. Municipality-year party-share vectors from presidential elections, with small parties folded into an "other" category.

Outputs. A 2-D or 3-D latent coordinate per municipality-year; a reconstruction error that flags outliers for the forensic capstone.

Training procedure. Adam optimizer on reconstruction MSE for the AE, and on the negative ELBO for the VAE. β-VAE variants weight the KL term to encourage disentangled latent axes. Multiple random seeds test stability.

Evaluation. Reconstruction MAE, silhouette of latent clusters against known regions, and seed-stability of the resulting map.

Electoral interpretation. Latent dimensions may correspond to axes such as "north–south industrial divide" or "rural–urban". Municipalities with high reconstruction error are structurally anomalous — candidates for the anomaly-forensics capstone, not automatically fraudulent.

Assumptions. (i) High-dimensional vote profiles lie near a low-dimensional manifold (manifold hypothesis). (ii) The latent axes are stable enough to be meaningful across cycles. (iii) Mixing multiple years in one space does not confound historical drift with geography.

Limitations. Latent dimensions arrive unlabeled; naming them requires manual correlation with covariates. VAEs may collapse if the KL term dominates; monitoring reconstructions is mandatory.

Card 4

Transformer — self-attention over electoral history

Selection rationale. A recurrent network must propagate memory through every cycle between the present and a distant past; a Transformer accesses any past cycle directly. This makes it a natural tool when the research question is interpretive as well as predictive: which past elections drive the current forecast?

What it does. A Transformer encoder builds a sequence representation using self-attention. Each position emits a query Q, a key K, and a value V; the output is a weighted sum of values, where the weight measures how much the query "attends" to each key. The dot products are scaled by √d_k so that softmax gradients do not vanish when dimension is large. Multi-Head Attention (MHA) runs several attention functions in parallel, allowing different heads to focus on different temporal regimes. Layer normalization stabilizes activations; residual connections let the network preserve raw features. Positional encoding injects order, which is implemented here as a learned or continuous ANIO embedding because electoral calendars are irregular. Causal masking blocks attention to future positions, preserving temporal causality.

Inputs. A sequence of cycle embeddings per unit: past party shares, lagged macroeconomics, structural indicators, and a time-stamp embedding.

Outputs. Next-cycle party shares, winner probabilities, and an attention matrix per head for interpretability.

Training procedure. Cross-entropy on the next cycle, with the same temporal split as the LSTM. Small d, few layers, and high dropout are used because n is small.

Evaluation. Same metrics as LSTM (MAE, winner accuracy, Brier). Attention maps are reported as a paper figure and treated as evidence, not as proof of importance.

Electoral interpretation. If attention concentrates on the immediately previous cycle, the system behaves like a smart persistence model. If it spreads across a whole sexenio, the model has learned longer-term regime dynamics.

Assumptions. (i) Past cycles are informative and the relevant ones can be identified by query-key similarity. (ii) Sequence length is small enough (T≈5–10) that quadratic attention cost is acceptable. (iii) The positional encoding captures true calendar distance.

Limitations. With only a handful of cycles per unit, Transformers overfit easily; the fair comparison is against the LSTM on the identical split. State-space models (Card 6) provide an efficiency alternative.

Card 5

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) / Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) — electoral memory

Selection rationale. Elections have memory: incumbent punishment, party stronghold inertia, sexenio effects. A recurrent model is the appropriate neural temporal baseline because it is expressive enough for these dynamics and small enough to fit our short, irregular panels.

What it does. A plain RNN (Recurrent Neural Network) updates a hidden state h_t = f(h_{t−1}, x_t), but gradients vanish over long spans. An LSTM adds a cell state c_t and three gates: an input gate decides what to write, a forget gate decides what to keep, and an output gate decides what to emit. A GRU merges the cell and hidden state into an update gate and a reset gate, using fewer parameters.

Inputs. Same cycle sequence as the Transformer (Card 4).

Outputs. Next-cycle party shares and winner class.

Training procedure. Minimize next-cycle CE with teacher forcing during training: the true previous output is fed as the next decoder input. Bidirectionality is forbidden because it would attend to future cycles and leak information.

Evaluation. MAE, winner accuracy, and a direct head-to-head comparison with the Transformer on the same split.

Electoral interpretation. The forget-gate values can be inspected to see whether the model treats the previous election or the previous sexenio as the dominant memory. The LSTM is the honest recurrent counterpart to the Transformer; the delta between them is a design datum.

Assumptions. (i) The relevant dynamics can be summarized by a hidden state vector. (ii) Causality is respected (no future information). (iii) There are enough sequences to learn the gates reliably.

Limitations. For local elections, sequences may contain only 1–3 cycles per unit. Pooling by entity or strong regularization is required; invented history is not allowed.

Card 6

State-Space Model (SSM) — efficient sequence alternative

Selection rationale. Transformers cost O(T²) in sequence length. For our short panels the absolute cost is modest, but comparing an SSM such as Mamba or TSMixer documents whether linear-time recurrence sacrifices accuracy for speed — a design datum, not an assumption.

What it does. A linear SSM (State-Space Model) defines a continuous or discrete dynamical system h_t = A·h_{t−1} + B·x_t, y_t = C·h_t. The state is updated in O(1) per step, so the whole sequence is processed in O(T). Selective variants such as Mamba make A, B, and C input-dependent, recovering content-aware behavior without attention.

Inputs. Identical cycle sequence to Cards 4 and 5.

Outputs. Next-cycle party shares and winner class.

Training procedure. Same objective as the LSTM/Transformer; compare wall-clock time and parameter count alongside predictive error.

Evaluation. MAE, winner accuracy, training time per epoch, and number of parameters versus the Transformer.

Electoral interpretation. If the SSM matches the Transformer, the extra quadratic cost of attention is unnecessary for this dataset and the simpler recurrent structure is preferred.

Assumptions. (i) The target dynamics are approximately linear in a learned latent state. (ii) Input-dependent transitions add enough flexibility for electoral memory.

Limitations. SSM libraries for time-series are less mature than Transformers; if implementation does not converge, the comparison is reported as a conceptual note with the attempted configuration.

Card 7

Graph Neural Network (GNN) / Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) / Graph Attention Network (GAT) — real geography

Selection rationale. A map is not a grid. Municipal borders are irregular and the relationship that matters for voting is contiguity and socioeconomic similarity. A GNN models municipalities as nodes and geographic or feature-based edges, letting information diffuse across the true topology. It replaces the CNN's artificial grid with a faithful graph operator.

What it does. A GNN (Graph Neural Network) updates each node's representation by aggregating messages from its neighbors. A GCN (Graph Convolutional Network) averages neighbor embeddings according to the normalized adjacency matrix. A GraphSAGE network samples and aggregates a fixed-size neighborhood. A GAT (Graph Attention Network) learns how much weight to give each neighbor, so some borders matter more than others. All variants share the core idea: similar connected units should have similar representations.

Inputs. Municipal graph: nodes = municipalities; edges = INEGI contiguity (or a documented k-NN fallback based on centroid distance or feature similarity). Node features = lagged shares, macro lag, VAE latent profile (Card 3).

Outputs. Updated node embeddings and predictions for next-cycle share/winner.

Training procedure. Semi-supervised: train on labeled election nodes, propagate through the graph via message passing. Use the same temporal split, with edges present only among units observed in the training period.

Evaluation. MAE and winner accuracy on local elections, compared with MLP and CNN. Ablation: GNN with contiguity edges vs. k-NN feature-similarity edges.

Electoral interpretation. The GNN tests whether geographic spillovers exist between neighboring municipalities. If the model improves, spatial diffusion is a real source of information; if not, geography is already captured by entity-level dummies.

Assumptions. (i) Adjacency is a meaningful proxy for information or behavioral diffusion. (ii) The graph is static or evolves slowly relative to electoral cycles. (iii) Boundary cases (islands, borders with other countries, disconnected municipalities) are handled explicitly.

Limitations. Building the adjacency requires official INEGI geometries; the fallback k-NN graph is an approximation and must be labeled as such. Edge definition directly influences results.

Card 8

Generative models — simulation and counterfactual illustration

Selection rationale. Generative models are pedagogically valuable for asking "what if" questions: what would 2024 look like with 2018 inflation? They are not used as predictions because the conditional distributions are not validated causally.

What it does. A GAN (Generative Adversarial Network) pits a generator G against a discriminator D in a minimax game; at equilibrium, G produces samples that D cannot distinguish from real data. A VAE decoder can also generate samples by feeding new latent points. Diffusion models gradually denoise random noise toward a data sample.

Inputs. Latent points or noise; conditional covariates such as a synthetic macro scenario.

Outputs. Synthetic municipality-year vote profiles.

Training procedure. Train the VAE (Card 3) first; use its decoder to generate profiles under perturbed macro inputs.

Evaluation. Visual inspection of latent traversals and distributional checks (party-share marginals, turnout). No predictive metrics are reported as findings.

Electoral interpretation. These outputs are explicitly labeled ILLUSTRATIVE. They help communicate uncertainty and sensitivity, not to claim a forecast.

Assumptions. (i) The latent distribution generalizes to unseen counterfactual conditions. (ii) The generative process is not causal.

Limitations. Without causal identification, generated profiles are speculations. They may inherit historical biases and produce implausible coalitions.

Card 9

Bag-of-Words (BoW) / Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) — textual floor

Selection rationale. Before using deep text models we must know whether simple word frequencies predict anything. BoW plus TF-IDF is transparent, fast, and interpretable; it sets the floor every neural method must beat or justify.

What it does. BoW represents a document as a vector of word counts, discarding order. TF-IDF (Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency) reweights each word by its rarity across the corpus: common words are downweighted, distinctive words are upweighted. The formula is TF-IDF(w,d) = count(w,d) · log(N / df_w), where N is the number of documents and df_w is the number of documents containing word w.

Inputs. Text of presidential interventions in a 90-day pre-election window, aggregated per entity (locals) or nationally (federals).

Outputs. Sparse feature vector per window, plus a linear or logistic prediction head for incumbent share or alternation.

Training procedure. Build the vocabulary on the temporal training set only; then vectorize all splits. A regularized linear model (logistic or ridge) serves as the text baseline.

Evaluation. MAE on incumbent share and Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) for alternation; top-weighted terms for interpretability.

Electoral interpretation. BoW tells us whether the frequency of words such as "seguridad", "empleo", or "corrupción" in the weeks before an election carries signal. If it does not, the extra capacity of LSTMs and BERT may be wasted.

Assumptions. (i) Word order does not matter much for this task. (ii) A fixed pre-election window captures the relevant discourse. (iii) TF-IDF vocabulary is stable across sexenios.

Limitations. Negation, irony, and context are lost. Political acronyms and neologisms may be split by a generic tokenizer.

Card 10

Text RNN / LSTM over political discourse

Selection rationale. If word order matters — for example, "no vamos a permitir" versus "vamos a permitir" — a recurrent text model should improve over BoW. The text RNN quantifies how much lexical order adds in this domain.

What it does. Each token is embedded into a dense vector; an LSTM or GRU reads the sequence and produces a final hidden state that summarizes the discourse. A dense head maps that state to the target.

Inputs. Tokenized intervention or aggregated daily discourse, aligned to the pre-election window.

Outputs. Incumbent share, alternation probability, or stance class.

Training procedure. Pre-trained or randomly initialized token embeddings; LSTM over the sequence; cross-entropy or MSE. Dropout on the recurrent output and the embedding layer.

Evaluation. Same as BoW; the head-to-head delta measures the value of sequence.

Electoral interpretation. A significant improvement means that phrasing, negation, and narrative structure carry information beyond mere word counts.

Assumptions. (i) The relevant signal is in the order of words within an intervention. (ii) The corpus is large enough to learn meaningful token embeddings.

Limitations. Long interventions and rare tokens make training unstable. The model has no access to broader context beyond the single input sequence.

Card 11

Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) tokenizer — domain morphology

Selection rationale. Mexican political Spanish contains acronyms and neologisms (INE, MORENA, IMSS, "chairos", "fifís", "mañanera") that a generic tokenizer may split incorrectly. Training a domain-specific tokenizer reduces the out-of-vocabulary rate and improves every downstream text model.

What it does. BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding) starts from characters and greedily merges the most frequent adjacent pair until a vocabulary of size B is reached. Frequent words become single tokens; rare words decompose into subwords. By construction, OOV (Out-Of-Vocabulary) tokens disappear, because any word can be spelled from subwords.

Inputs. The full mañaneras corpus.

Outputs. A vocabulary file and tokenization statistics: OOV rate against a generic Spanish tokenizer, mean tokens per word, acronym coverage.

Training procedure. Count pairs, merge, repeat; evaluate on held-out political interventions.

Evaluation. OOV rate, tokenization stability, and downstream task improvement (LSTM or BERT fine-tuning).

Electoral interpretation. A specialized tokenizer is a measurable investment: if political acronyms are not treated as atomic units, the model misses a core feature of Mexican discourse.

Assumptions. (i) The corpus is large and representative enough to learn stable merges. (ii) The vocabulary size B balances compactness and coverage.

Limitations. BPE is greedy and deterministic; it cannot handle new acronyms after training. Comparison with a pre-trained Spanish tokenizer is mandatory.

Card 12

N-gram language models — rhetorical regime change

Selection rationale. N-grams are the simplest language model. Comparing cross-sexenio perplexity quantifies how much the presidential vocabulary changed from one administration to the next — a proxy for rhetorical regime change.

What it does. An n-gram model estimates the probability of a word given the previous n−1 words, P(w_t | w_{t−n+1} … w_{t−1}), from smoothed counts. Perplexity is the exponential of the average negative log-probability: exp(−(1/N) Σ log P(w_t)). Lower perplexity means the model finds the text more predictable.

Inputs. Tokenized corpus split by sexenio.

Outputs. Per-sexenio perplexity and a cross-sexenio perplexity matrix; derived feature drift_retorico_sexenio.

Training procedure. Estimate n-gram counts (n=2,3,4) with Kneser–Ney or Laplace smoothing on one sexenio; evaluate perplexity on another.

Evaluation. Cross-perplexity heatmap; stability against random train/test splits.

Electoral interpretation. If a 2024 model trained on 2018 discourse has high perplexity, the rhetorical regime has shifted. This is a covariate of possible electoral realignment.

Assumptions. (i) Vocabulary and style evolve slowly enough to model per sexenio. (ii) Predictability correlates with rhetorical continuity.

Limitations. N-grams ignore long-range syntax and semantics. Perplexity is not a causal predictor.

Card 13

Word embeddings — semantic geometry of the political lexicon

Selection rationale. Embeddings turn political concepts into vectors such that related words are close and analogies are arithmetic. They let us measure semantic drift across sexenios (e.g., does "MORENA" move toward where "PRD" used to be?).

What it does. Word2Vec skip-gram learns vectors by predicting the context words around a target word; words that share contexts end up close in the embedding space. GloVe (Global Vectors) factorizes the log co-occurrence matrix instead. The geometry supports analogies such as v_rey − v_hombre + v_mujer ≈ v_reina.

Inputs. Tokenized mañaneras corpus, possibly split by sexenio.

Outputs. Dense vectors (e.g., 100-dimensional) for each word, and a t-SNE/UMAP map for visualization.

Training procedure. Train skip-gram with negative sampling or GloVe on the corpus; evaluate on word-similarity tasks for key political terms.

Evaluation. Cosine-similarity rankings of known related terms; visualization quality; downstream classification improvement over BoW.

Electoral interpretation. Embedding drift tracks how the political lexicon reorganizes between administrations. A party embedding that moves closer to "pobreza" or "transformación" signals a repositioning in the agenda.

Assumptions. (i) Context predicts meaning well enough in political speech. (ii) The corpus is large enough to learn stable vectors. (iii) Analogies in the political domain are approximately linear.

Limitations. Embeddings are static and do not handle polysemy or context; a pre-trained contextual encoder (BETO, Card 17) addresses this.

Card 14

t-SNE / UMAP — visualizing high-dimensional semantics

Selection rationale. Human readers cannot inspect 100-dimensional vectors directly. Dimensionality reduction maps them to 2-D for visualization, with the caveat that global distances are distorted.

What it does. t-SNE (t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding) minimizes the KL divergence between pairwise similarity distributions in the original high-dimensional space and the 2-D map. UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) assumes the data lie on a manifold and preserves both local and more global structure, often faster than t-SNE.

Inputs. Word embeddings, municipality latent profiles (Card 3), or BETO sentence embeddings.

Outputs. 2-D scatter plots colored by word type, party, sexenio, or geographic region.

Training procedure. Run t-SNE or UMAP with multiple perplexity/neighbor settings; report the most stable one.

Evaluation. Visual inspection and nearest-neighbor preservation metrics.

Electoral interpretation. Clusters in the map reveal conceptual neighborhoods — for example, economic terms, security terms, or regional references. The map is a communication tool, not a quantitative distance metric.

Assumptions. (i) Local neighborhoods in high-D are meaningful. (ii) A 2-D embedding preserves enough structure to be useful.

Limitations. Global distances in t-SNE/UMAP are not interpretable. Different hyperparameters produce different layouts; stability checks are required.

Card 15

Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) — the presidential agenda as a variable

Selection rationale. Agenda-setting theory says that what the government talks about influences what citizens think about. LDA turns the mañaneras corpus into a time series of topic prevalences that can be merged with the electoral data as a structural covariate.

What it does. LDA is a generative probabilistic topic model. Each document draws a topic mixture θ_d ~ Dirichlet(α); each topic is a word distribution φ_k ~ Dirichlet(β); each observed word picks a topic and then a word from that topic. Inference recovers the θ and φ distributions. Topic quality is measured by C_v coherence, which compares the semantic similarity of a topic's top words.

Inputs. Daily or weekly bags of words from the mañaneras corpus.

Outputs. K topic-word distributions and a time series of topic prevalence share_topic_k per week.

Training procedure. Fit LDA for K ∈ {10, 20, 30, 50} on the training corpus; select K by C_v coherence; apply the trained model to all windows.

Evaluation. C_v coherence, manual topic labels, and predictive correlation with incumbent support or turnout.

Electoral interpretation. A rising prevalence of security discourse before a state election may depress turnout or favor the opposition; the topic-share series makes that hypothesis testable.

Assumptions. (i) Documents are mixtures of a small number of topics. (ii) The Dirichlet prior is appropriate for discourse. (iii) Topics are stable across the training period.

Limitations. LDA is a bag-of-words model: it ignores word order and context. The number of topics K must be chosen by the analyst.

Card 16

Structural Topic Model (STM) — topics conditioned on context

Selection rationale. LDA ignores document metadata. STM makes topic prevalence a function of covariates (sexenio, pre-electoral quarter, inflation), yielding regression coefficients that can be interpreted substantively: for example, "one extra inflation point corresponds to a Δ increase in economy-topic discourse."

What it does. STM generalizes LDA by drawing the topic-mixture parameter from a logistic-normal distribution whose mean is a linear regression Γ·x_d on document covariates. The Γ coefficients are directly interpretable. Topic content can also depend on covariates.

Inputs. Same corpus as LDA plus document-level covariates: year, sexenio, pre-electoral indicator, and contemporaneous macro variables.

Outputs. Topic prevalence series and a table of Γ coefficients with confidence intervals.

Training procedure. Fit the STM package (R) via rpy2 or a separate documented script; inspect convergence diagnostics.

Evaluation. Coherence, coefficient significance, and comparison with LDA on a held-out prediction task.

Electoral interpretation. STM turns the agenda from a black-box cluster into a measurable response to economic and political context. It produces coefficients, not just clusters.

Assumptions. (i) Topic prevalence is smoothly related to covariates. (ii) The logistic-normal link is appropriate. (iii) Covariates are not collinear.

Limitations. STM is more computationally demanding than LDA and requires careful covariate construction. It is fit in R, so the notebook must document the R–Python bridge.

Card 17

Named Entity Recognition (NER) and stance classification — territorial attention

Selection rationale. Two electoral questions require text understanding: (a) what is the president's tone toward opposition, economy, and security? and (b) which states and municipalities does the president mention? The latter is a unique feature enabled by the mañaneras corpus: presidential territorial attention.

What it does. NER assigns each token a BIO tag: B-X marks the beginning of an entity of type X, I-X its inside, and O means outside any entity. Entity types are typically PER (person), ORG (organization), LOC (location), and GPE (geopolitical entity). Stance classification assigns a document or span to a label such as positive, negative, neutral, or combative toward a target.

Inputs. Sentence-level or intervention-level text from the mañaneras corpus.

Outputs. Lists of normalized entity mentions per date and a stance label per relevant span; weekly aggregation atencion_presidencial_ent.

Training procedure. Fine-tune a Spanish/multilingual BERT-NER model on a labeled sample, or use an off-the-shelf Spanish NER model with a gazetteer that maps mentions to the canonical entity key. Stance requires an annotated sample (n≥500) with inter-annotator agreement or LLM-assisted validation.

Evaluation. Entity-level F1 for NER; macro-F1 for stance. Manual error analysis on the most frequent entities.

Electoral interpretation. If the president mentions a state more frequently in the months before its gubernatorial election, attention itself becomes a covariate. Stance tracks the tonal climate around key issues.

Assumptions. (i) Entity mentions can be normalized reliably to electoral geography. (ii) Stance labels are stable across annotators. (iii) Attention is measured by mention frequency, which is a coarse proxy.

Limitations. NER models may miss colloquial place names and abbreviations. Stance annotation is subjective and must be reported with agreement metrics.

Card 18

Conditional Random Field (CRF) — interpretable sequence baseline

Selection rationale. A CRF is the classical, interpretable baseline for NER. Before declaring that a deep Transformer NER is necessary, we measure whether a linear-chain CRF with hand-crafted features already solves most of the problem.

What it does. A CRF is a discriminative undirected graphical model over a sequence. It models P(y|x) ∝ exp(Σ_t [s(y_t,x,t) + A_{y_{t−1},y_t}]), where s scores the emission features of tag y_t at position t and A is the learned transition matrix that encodes which tags may follow which. Decoding uses the Viterbi algorithm.

Inputs. Tokens with orthographic features (capitalization, prefixes, suffixes, ±2 word context).

Outputs. BIO tag sequence and the interpretable transition matrix.

Training procedure. Train sklearn-crfsuite on a labeled NER sample; tune regularization on a development set.

Evaluation. Entity-level F1 against the same test set used for the Transformer NER.

Electoral interpretation. The transition matrix itself is a deliverable: it shows, for example, that a location tag is unlikely to follow a person tag. This transparency is the reason the CRF is retained even after a deep model is trained.

Assumptions. (i) Tag transitions are the main source of structure. (ii) Local orthographic features are sufficient to identify entities.

Limitations. CRFs do not share parameters across words and cannot leverage subword or contextual semantics; hence they are expected to underperform BETO-NER on rare mentions.

Card 19

BETO / BERT — contextual Spanish encoder for fusion

Selection rationale. BETO is the Spanish BERT model. It produces contextual embeddings that capture word meaning in context and subword structure, solving many of the limitations of static embeddings and bag-of-words models. Its [CLS] token or mean-pooled sentence embedding feeds the multimodal fusion in Part V.

What it does. BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) is a bidirectional Transformer encoder pre-trained on Spanish text with MLM (Masked Language Modeling): random tokens are hidden and the model learns to predict them. BETO is the Spanish adaptation. Fine-tuning adds a task-specific head on top of the pre-trained body.

Inputs. Intervention text or aggregated daily discourse chunks.

Outputs. A 768-dimensional vector per input, optionally a classification score for stance or NER.

Training procedure. Fine-tune BETO on stance classification and/or NER with a small learning rate; freeze lower layers if overfitting. Persist embeddings to texto_emb.parquet for fusion.

Evaluation. Downstream task F1; ablation of domain fine-tuning versus generic BETO pool; fusion improvement in Part V.

Electoral interpretation. BETO embeddings encode the contextual meaning of the discourse at each date. They are the textual branch's contribution to the multimodal model and the final input for textual nowcasting.

Assumptions. (i) Spanish political discourse is close enough to the BETO pre-training distribution. (ii) Fine-tuning data are representative and not leaked.

Limitations. BERT is computationally expensive; embeddings are computed once and reused. It may struggle with highly colloquial political jargon absent from pre-training.

Card 20

Late-fusion multimodal model — combining every channel

Selection rationale. Mexican elections are a multimodal problem: votes (tabular), history (temporal), geography (spatial), and discourse (text). Late fusion keeps each modality's encoder independent and auditable, then learns only the combination. This avoids forcing incompatible geometries (vectors, sequences, graphs, text) into a single early representation and makes ablations clean.

What it does. Each branch (MLP tabular, LSTM temporal, GNN spatial, BETO textual) is trained on its own task and then frozen. Each emits a fixed-length embedding. These are concatenated with election and geography embeddings and fed into a small fusion MLP. The fusion head predicts shares, winner, and turnout.

Inputs. For each target election: tabular features, the unit's past cycle sequence, the municipal graph context, and the pre-election textual representation.

Outputs. Party shares (softmax), winner class, scalar turnout, and uncertainty via Monte Carlo dropout or an ensemble.

Training procedure. Train each branch separately; freeze; train the fusion head with the same temporal split. Then run mandatory ablations: tabular only, +temporal, +spatial, +text, full. Report each channel's marginal Δ over the tabular baseline.

Evaluation. Full metric suite (MAE, winner accuracy, Brier, ECE) per ablation. The key result is not the full model's absolute score but the incremental value of each channel.

Electoral interpretation. The fusion answers the thesis question: how much does each mode of information — past votes, time, geography, presidential discourse — add to the prediction of Mexican elections? A negative ablation is also a finding.

Assumptions. (i) Channels provide complementary, not redundant, information. (ii) Freezing branches preserves their individual meaning. (iii) The fusion head is small enough to avoid overfitting the limited panel.

Limitations. The model is only as good as the weakest link; missing text data or an approximate adjacency graph limit the full fusion. Causal interpretation is forbidden.

Part VIII

Pedagogical deep-dive: theoretical frameworks with everyday analogies

This part expands every theoretical framework from Parts III and IV into a self-contained explanation. Each segment assumes no prior knowledge, expands every acronym at first use, explains the core mechanism from first principles, and provides an everyday analogy. It is meant to be read before the corresponding notebook, so that the reader understands why the model exists and how it maps onto the Mexican electoral dataset. SYLLABUS-CONFIRMED

Topic

1. Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)

A multilayer perceptron (MLP) is a feed-forward neural network that transforms an input through layers of artificial neurons. Each neuron computes a weighted sum, z=w^{T}x+b, then applies an activation function. Without nonlinear activations, stacked layers would collapse into one linear transformation. Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) outputs max(0,z), making optimization efficient. Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) smoothly weights inputs according to their magnitude and is common in modern language models. For multiclass prediction, softmax converts output scores into probabilities that sum to one.

Training minimizes a loss function. Backpropagation applies the chain rule to calculate how each weight contributed to the error; gradient descent then updates weights in the opposite direction of the gradient. The universal approximation theorem states that a sufficiently wide MLP can approximate any continuous function on a bounded domain, although it does not guarantee efficient learning or good generalization.[1]

Analogy: an MLP resembles a sequence of committee meetings: each layer summarizes information, nonlinear decisions reshape it, and feedback identifies which judgments caused the final mistake.

Key papers: Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams (1986); Cybenko (1989).

Topic

2. PyTorch and Automatic Differentiation

PyTorch is a machine-learning framework in which data are represented as tensors: multidimensional arrays that can run efficiently on central processing units or graphics processing units. A model applies tensor operations to produce predictions, and a loss function measures prediction error.

PyTorch’s automatic differentiation (autograd) records the operations used to compute the output. When the loss is evaluated, autograd traverses this computational graph backward and calculates derivatives with the chain rule. This removes the need to derive gradients manually. An optimizer then changes parameters using those gradients.

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) estimates the gradient from a randomly selected mini-batch rather than the entire dataset. This makes training faster and introduces useful noise. Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) combines momentum—an average of recent gradients—with an estimate of squared gradients, allowing each parameter to receive an individually scaled update.

Analogy: tensors are ingredients, the computational graph is a recipe, autograd retraces the recipe to identify which ingredient caused an undesirable taste, and the optimizer adjusts the ingredients.

A practical training cycle is: compute predictions, calculate loss, clear old gradients, backpropagate, update parameters, and repeat.

Key papers: Baydin et al. (2018); Kingma and Ba (2015).

Topic

3. Regularization

Regularization reduces overfitting: the tendency to memorize training examples while performing poorly on new data. The bias–variance tradeoff describes two types of error. High bias reflects an overly simple model that misses real patterns; high variance reflects a model that reacts excessively to the particular training sample. Regularization usually accepts a little more bias to reduce variance.

L2 weight decay adds a penalty proportional to the squared weights to the loss. Large weights are discouraged, producing smoother functions. Dropout randomly disables units during training, preventing the network from relying on a small group of features. At inference time, all units are used with appropriate scaling. Early stopping monitors validation performance and halts training when validation error begins to rise, before the model memorizes noise.

Analogy: learning a campaign message from survey responses is like studying for an exam. Memorizing every practice question gives high training performance but fails on new questions; learning broad principles generalizes better. Regularization acts like an instructor who discourages memorization and stops practice at the right time.

The appropriate strength depends on dataset size, noise, model capacity, and the validation design. In electoral research, regularization is especially important when many linguistic or demographic features are available relative to the number of observations.

Key papers: Hoerl and Kennard (1970); Srivastava et al. (2014).

Topic

4. Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)

A convolutional neural network (CNN) detects local patterns and combines them into larger structures. A convolutional filter, or kernel, slides across an input and computes weighted sums of nearby values. For images, the values are pixels; for text, they may represent neighboring word or character embeddings. Each filter learns to respond to a particular pattern, such as an edge, phrase, or visual shape.

Weight sharing means the same filter is used at every location. This greatly reduces parameters and makes detection relatively insensitive to where a pattern occurs. A neuron’s receptive field is the portion of the original input that can influence it. Stacking convolutions expands receptive fields, allowing the network to combine simple local patterns into complex ones.

Pooling, such as maximum pooling, summarizes nearby activations and reduces spatial or sequential resolution. This can improve computational efficiency and modestly increase robustness to small shifts, although excessive pooling may discard important information.

Analogy: a CNN is like scanning a newspaper with the same highlighter rule everywhere: first mark words, then phrases, then recurring arguments. Local evidence is gradually assembled into a document-level interpretation.

For electoral text, one-dimensional CNNs can identify short expressions associated with topics, sentiment, or political frames, but their locality can limit long-range contextual understanding.

Key papers: LeCun et al. (1998); LeCun, Bengio, and Hinton (2015).[2]

Topic

5. Transfer Learning

Transfer learning reuses knowledge learned from one task or dataset for another. During pre-training, a model learns general representations from a large source corpus, often through supervised prediction or self-supervised objectives. During fine-tuning, its parameters are adapted using the target dataset.

Researchers may freeze some layers, meaning their parameters remain unchanged, while training only a task-specific output layer or later layers. Freezing is useful when the target dataset is small; updating more layers can help when the target task differs substantially from the source.

A central difficulty is domain shift: the source and target data follow different distributions. For example, a model trained on international news may encounter different vocabulary, political institutions, slang, or campaign conventions in Mexican electoral discourse. Transfer is beneficial when useful structures carry over, but harmful transfer, or negative transfer, can occur when source knowledge is misleading.

Analogy: a translator who already understands Spanish literature can learn campaign language faster than a beginner, but must adapt to regional idioms, political slogans, and institutional terminology.

Evaluation should compare transferred models with models trained from scratch and use geographically or temporally appropriate validation splits. Fine-tuning can also inherit biases from the pre-training corpus.

Key papers: Bengio (2012); Pan and Yang (2010).

Topic

6. Autoencoder and Variational Autoencoder

An autoencoder (AE) learns to compress an input into a lower-dimensional bottleneck and reconstruct it. The encoder maps an observation to a latent representation; the decoder maps that representation back to an approximation of the original. This reflects the manifold hypothesis: high-dimensional observations may lie near a lower-dimensional structure, such as recurring political themes underlying many text features.

A variational autoencoder (VAE) makes the latent representation probabilistic. Instead of assigning one code, the encoder predicts a distribution—typically a Gaussian—with a mean and variance. Training balances reconstruction quality with a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence penalty that encourages the learned distribution to remain near a chosen prior, usually a standard normal distribution. The combined objective is the evidence lower bound (ELBO).

The reparameterization trick writes a latent sample as z=μ+σε, where ε is random noise. This separates randomness from learnable parameters, allowing gradients to pass through the sampling operation.

Analogy: an AE compresses a long speech into notes and reconstructs it; a VAE learns a smooth map of possible speeches, so nearby latent points produce related outputs.

VAEs are useful for representation learning, visualization, simulation, and detecting unusual observations, but generated outputs may be blurry or overly regularized.

Key papers: Hinton and Salakhutdinov (2006); Kingma and Welling (2014).[3]

Topic

7. Transformer

A Transformer processes sequences primarily through self-attention. For each token, it creates a query, key, and value. Scaled dot-product attention compares a query with all keys, divides by the square root of the key dimension for numerical stability, applies softmax, and computes a weighted sum of values:

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax({QK^{T}}{√{d_k}})V.

Multi-head attention runs several attention mechanisms in parallel, allowing different heads to capture different relationships, such as agreement, topic connections, or long-distance dependencies. Because attention alone does not encode order, positional encoding supplies information about token positions. Causal masking prevents a token from attending to future tokens during autoregressive generation.

Each attention and feed-forward sublayer is surrounded by residual connections, which add the input back to the transformed output, and LayerNorm (Layer Normalization), which stabilizes activations and optimization.

Analogy: a committee member deciding how to interpret one sentence consults every other word, giving more attention to relevant words; multiple committee members specialize in different relationships.

Transformers capture long-range context more directly than CNNs, but self-attention can be computationally expensive for long sequences and may reproduce biases in training data.

Key papers: Vaswani et al. (2017); Devlin et al. (2019).

Topic

8. Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR)

Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) converts spoken audio into text. First, the waveform is divided into short overlapping frames. A Mel spectrogram represents the energy in each frame across frequencies mapped to the Mel scale, which approximates how humans perceive pitch. This transforms a long audio signal into a two-dimensional pattern that a neural network can process. An encoder converts these acoustic features into hidden representations; a decoder then predicts the most probable sequence of characters, subwords, or words. In an encoder–decoder system, the decoder uses both its previous prediction and the encoder’s representation to produce the next token. For electoral research, ASR can transcribe rallies, interviews, debates, and citizen recordings, but accents, code-switching, noise, and names can reduce accuracy. Performance is commonly measured with Word Error Rate (WER):

WER={S+D+I}{N}

where S is substitutions, D deletions, I insertions, and N reference words. Lower WER is better. An everyday analogy is a listener turning speech into notes while repeatedly checking the surrounding context. Key references are Graves et al. (2006) on connectionist temporal classification and Chan et al. (2016) on attention-based speech recognition.

Topic

9. LSTM / GRU / seq2seq

A Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) processes a sequence one element at a time while carrying information from earlier steps. This is useful for language and speech, but ordinary RNNs can lose older information because repeated multiplication causes gradients to shrink or explode. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks address this with gates: the forget gate removes irrelevant information, the input gate writes new information, and the output gate exposes selected memory. A Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) uses a simpler update gate and reset gate, often with fewer parameters. In sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) learning, an encoder summarizes an input sequence and a decoder generates an output sequence, such as turning an interview into a label sequence or translation. During training, teacher forcing gives the decoder the correct previous token rather than its own possibly incorrect prediction. This speeds learning but can create a train–test mismatch, because deployment requires the model to use its own outputs. The everyday analogy is a secretary who keeps a notebook, crosses out outdated facts, and decides which notes to consult before writing each sentence. Foundational references are Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997) for LSTM and Cho et al. (2014) for GRU and encoder–decoder learning.

Topic

10. Large Language Models (LLM)

A Large Language Model (LLM) learns statistical patterns in text and generates language by predicting the next token, where a token may be a word, word fragment, punctuation mark, or symbol. During training, the model receives a sequence such as “The candidate spoke at the” and learns to assign high probability to likely continuations such as “debate.” Repeating this objective across enormous datasets teaches grammar, factual associations, style, and some reasoning-like patterns. Scale refers primarily to the number of parameters, training data, and computation; increasing these often improves capability, although performance also depends on data quality, architecture, and evaluation. LLMs do not automatically verify truth. Hallucination occurs when a model produces fluent but unsupported or false content because it is optimizing plausible continuation rather than consulting reality. In electoral research, hallucinations can invent quotations, misidentify candidates, or confuse dates and institutions, so outputs require source checking. An everyday analogy is an exceptionally well-read autocomplete system: it can complete a sentence convincingly but may not know whether the claim is true. Important references include Vaswani et al. (2017), which introduced the Transformer architecture, and Brown et al. (2020), which demonstrated large-scale few-shot language modeling.

Topic

11. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) combines a language generator with an external document-search system. Instead of asking a model to answer solely from parameters learned during training, the system first retrieves relevant passages from a collection such as electoral laws, official results, manifestos, or interview transcripts. In dense retrieval, a neural encoder converts both the query and documents into numerical vectors called embeddings. Similarity—often cosine similarity—selects passages whose meanings are close to the query, even when they do not share exact words. The language model then receives the retrieved passages as context and generates an answer. RAG can improve freshness, domain coverage, and traceability, but retrieval errors still matter: irrelevant, incomplete, biased, or outdated documents can lead to incorrect answers. Faithfulness means that the generated response is supported by the retrieved evidence rather than merely sounding plausible. An everyday analogy is asking a researcher to answer only after searching a labeled filing cabinet and citing the documents consulted. In a Mexican electoral project, each answer should preserve document identifiers, dates, page numbers, and quoted evidence where possible. The foundational paper is Lewis et al. (2020), which introduced RAG, while Karpukhin et al. (2020) established dense passage retrieval.

Topic

12. Agentic Workflows

An agentic workflow is a system in which a language model plans and performs multiple steps rather than producing a single response. It may interpret a question, select a tool, execute a database query, inspect the result, revise its plan, and produce a final answer. Tool use means giving the model controlled access to capabilities such as search, statistical software, geographic information systems, speech transcription, or a document database. The model should not be treated as an unrestricted autonomous decision-maker: tools need permissions, validation rules, and human oversight. An audit trail records the input, model version, prompt, retrieved sources, tool calls, intermediate outputs, errors, and final result. This is essential in electoral research because analysts may need to reproduce a finding or explain how a classification was made. An everyday analogy is a research assistant who receives a question, consults official records, calculates statistics, asks for clarification when necessary, and keeps a work diary. Agentic systems can improve productivity but may compound errors: a mistaken early assumption can influence every later step. Relevant conceptual references include Yao et al. (2023) on ReAct—reasoning combined with acting—and Schick et al. (2023) on language models learning to use tools.

Topic

13. Generative Adversarial Networks and Diffusion

A Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) contains two competing models. The generator creates synthetic examples, while the discriminator tries to distinguish generated examples from real ones. Training follows a minimax objective: the generator seeks to fool the discriminator, and the discriminator seeks to classify correctly. Over time, the generator may learn to produce realistic images, audio, or tabular records. GANs can support counterfactual simulation—for example, exploring how speech or imagery might appear under altered demographic or campaign conditions—but synthetic outputs are not evidence of what actually occurred and can reproduce training-data bias. Diffusion models use a different process. During training, controlled noise is gradually added to real data; the model learns to reverse this process and generate a clean sample from noise. Diffusion usually offers more stable and diverse generation than GANs, though it can be computationally expensive. An everyday analogy is comparing a forger competing with an inspector (GAN) to an artist learning how to restore a photograph after progressively heavier blurring (diffusion). In electoral studies, synthetic data should be labeled clearly and validated against privacy, realism, and fairness criteria. Key references are Goodfellow et al. (2014) for GANs and Ho et al. (2020) for denoising diffusion probabilistic models.

Topic

14. State-Space Models (SSM)

A State-Space Model (SSM) represents a sequence through a hidden state that changes over time. In a simple linear recurrence,

x_t=Ax_{t-1}+Bu_t,\qquad y_t=Cx_t,

the current state x_t depends on the previous state and input u_t, while y_t is the observable output. This creates a compact memory of the past. Unlike self-attention, which directly compares many pairs of positions and can require quadratic computation for long sequences, an SSM updates information through recurrence and can scale approximately linearly with sequence length. Mamba is a selective SSM: its parameters depend on the input, allowing it to retain important events and suppress irrelevant ones. TSMixer, by contrast, is an all-Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) architecture that mixes information across time and features; it is not itself an SSM, but it is a relevant efficient alternative for time-series forecasting. For electoral research, these models can represent polling trajectories, turnout patterns, media volume, or campaign events over time. An everyday analogy is a notebook whose current summary is updated after each day, while attention is like rereading every previous page whenever writing a new entry. Key references are Gu et al. (2022) on structured SSMs, Gu and Dao (2023) on Mamba, and Chen et al. (2023) on TSMixer.[1]

Topic

15. Bag-of-Words (BoW) / Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF)

Bag-of-Words (BoW) represents a document as a vector of word counts. First, the corpus vocabulary is listed; then each document receives one numerical value per vocabulary item. Word order is discarded, so “candidate attacks policy” and “policy attacks candidate” receive the same basic representation. This makes BoW simple and interpretable for electoral texts, such as counting mentions of security, corruption, or jobs. Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) improves this representation by weighting words according to both their importance within a document and their rarity across the corpus. A common form is \mathrm{TF\text{-}IDF}(t,d)=\mathrm{TF}(t,d)×log(N/\mathrm{DF}(t)), where N is the number of documents and \mathrm{DF}(t) is the number containing term t. Frequent but uninformative words receive lower weights.

An everyday analogy is comparing newspapers by counting their words, while giving extra attention to words that distinguish one newspaper from many others.

Key papers include Salton and Buckley (1988) on term weighting and Robertson (2004) on probabilistic relevance and inverse document frequency. TF-IDF is useful for transparent baselines, but it cannot reliably capture negation, sarcasm, or word order.

Topic

16. Text Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) / Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) over tokens

A Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) processes a sequence one token at a time while maintaining a hidden state: a numerical summary of what it has read. For a token sequence x_1,\ldots,x_T, the model repeatedly updates h_t=f(x_t,h_{t-1}). The hidden state can then classify a message, predict its next token, or estimate political sentiment. The difficulty is that ordinary RNNs may lose information over long sequences because gradients become extremely small or large during training. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks address this with a memory cell and gates: the input gate controls what information enters, the forget gate controls what is removed, and the output gate controls what is exposed. These mechanisms help preserve relevant evidence across a long post or speech.

An everyday analogy is reading a speech with a notebook: each sentence updates your notes, while erasers and bookmarks determine what to forget and remember.

Foundational papers are Bengio et al. (2003), which introduced neural probabilistic language modeling, and Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997), which introduced LSTM. RNNs require ordered tokens and can model context better than BoW, although modern Transformer models often outperform them.

Topic

17. Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) tokenizer and Out-of-Vocabulary (OOV)

Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) is a subword-tokenization method designed to represent both common words and rare or previously unseen forms. It begins with small units—characters or bytes—and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair until reaching a chosen vocabulary size. For example, frequent pieces such as “polít” and “ica” may become reusable subwords. A word is therefore represented as several learned units rather than requiring one vocabulary entry for the entire word. This is valuable in Mexican electoral data, where names, hashtags, abbreviations, misspellings, and Spanish morphology produce many rare forms.

An Out-of-Vocabulary (OOV) item is a word or symbol absent from a model’s fixed vocabulary. A whole-word tokenizer may replace it with an unknown token, losing information. BPE reduces this problem because unfamiliar words can usually be decomposed into known subwords, producing an effectively open vocabulary. Its main trade-off is that segmentation can be linguistically awkward and sequences may become longer.

An everyday analogy is assembling an unfamiliar long word from familiar Lego pieces instead of declaring the entire word unusable.

The key paper is Sennrich, Haddow, and Birch (2016), which adapted BPE for neural machine translation of rare words. The method originates in Gage (1994) as a data-compression algorithm.

Topic

18. N-gram language models and perplexity

An N-gram language model estimates the probability of a token from the previous n-1 tokens. A bigram model uses one previous token; a trigram uses two. Formally, it approximates P(w_t| w_1,\ldots,w_{t-1}) with P(w_t| w_{t-n+1},\ldots,w_{t-1}), making a limited-history or Markov assumption. Probabilities are learned from counts in a corpus. Because unseen sequences receive zero probability, practical models use smoothing, such as adding small counts or interpolating different n-gram orders.

Perplexity measures how surprised a model is by a test sequence. It is the exponentiated average negative log probability:

\mathrm{PP}(w_{1:T})= exp(-{1}{T}Σ_{t=1}^{T}log P(w_t| w_{<t})).

Lower perplexity indicates better predictive performance on comparable data, though it does not automatically mean better political interpretation or classification.

An everyday analogy is predicting the next word in a conversation using only the last few words; a low perplexity means the conversation is not very surprising to you.

Important papers include Shannon (1948), which established probabilistic information-theoretic approaches to language, and Katz (1987), which introduced influential smoothing for n-gram models. N-grams are computationally simple and interpretable but struggle with long-distance dependencies.

Topic

19. Word embeddings: Word2Vec / GloVe

Word embeddings represent words as dense numerical vectors in which geometrically similar words tend to have similar meanings. This follows the distributional hypothesis: words appearing in similar contexts tend to have related meanings. Instead of counting each word independently, an embedding model learns coordinates from context patterns.

Word2Vec, introduced by Mikolov et al. (2013), uses a neural objective. In the Continuous Bag-of-Words model, surrounding words predict a target word; in the Skip-gram model, a target predicts nearby words. Training adjusts vectors so that useful word-context relationships become close in vector space. Global Vectors for Word Representation (GloVe), proposed by Pennington et al. (2014), learns vectors from global word co-occurrence statistics while preserving important local relationships. Both methods can reveal associations among electoral terms, parties, candidates, and issues, but these associations may reflect social biases in the training corpus rather than objective facts.

An everyday analogy is constructing a map from neighborhoods: places visited by similar people or activities appear near one another.

Key papers are Mikolov et al. (2013) for Word2Vec and Pennington, Socher, and Manning (2014) for GloVe. Static embeddings assign one vector per word, so they cannot fully distinguish meanings that change with context.

Topic

20. t-SNE / UMAP for visualization

t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) are nonlinear dimensionality-reduction methods. They convert high-dimensional representations—such as document embeddings or hidden states—into two- or three-dimensional coordinates for visualization. They do not primarily create better features for prediction; they create maps that help researchers inspect clusters, outliers, and possible differences among parties, regions, or topics.

t-SNE constructs probabilities representing neighborhood relationships in the original space and seeks a low-dimensional map with similar relationships, using a heavy-tailed Student-t distribution to reduce the crowding problem. It usually emphasizes local neighborhoods, but distances between separated clusters and the apparent size of clusters can be misleading. UMAP models a neighborhood graph using assumptions from manifold learning and optimizes a low-dimensional representation. It often preserves more global structure and scales better to large datasets, although its output depends on parameter choices and random initialization.

An everyday analogy is drawing a subway map: nearby stations remain connected and legible, but geographic distances and directions may be distorted.

The foundational papers are van der Maaten and Hinton (2008) for t-SNE and McInnes, Healy, and Melville (2018) for UMAP. Visual clusters should be treated as exploratory evidence, not definitive proof of ideological or semantic categories.

Topic

21. Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) Topic Modeling

Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) is an unsupervised probabilistic method for discovering hidden themes, or topics, in a collection of documents. “Latent” means unobserved, while “Dirichlet” refers to the probability distribution used to represent how strongly topics appear in documents. LDA assumes that each document contains a mixture of topics and that each topic is a probability distribution over words (Blei, Ng, and Jordan 2003). For example, a campaign speech might be 50% about security, 30% about the economy, and 20% about social programs. The model observes only words and estimates these hidden topic mixtures from recurring patterns of word co-occurrence. Researchers choose the number of topics in advance, and the resulting topics must be interpreted by examining their most probable words and representative documents. LDA is useful for exploring what Mexican electoral texts discuss, but its topics are not automatically meaningful labels and may require substantive validation.

Everyday analogy: Imagine sorting a large box of mixed newspaper clippings without titles. By noticing which words repeatedly appear together—such as “jobs,” “wages,” and “growth”—you infer an “economy” pile, even though no pile was labeled beforehand.

Key paper: Blei, Ng, and Jordan (2003).

Topic

22. Structural Topic Model (STM)

The Structural Topic Model (STM) extends Latent Dirichlet Allocation by incorporating document-level metadata—information such as party, speaker, date, region, or platform. Like LDA, it represents each document as a mixture of topics and each topic as a distribution over words. Its distinctive feature is that metadata can help explain two aspects of text: topic prevalence, or how much a topic appears in a document, and topic content, or which words are used to express that topic (Roberts et al. 2014). For example, an STM could estimate whether immigration is discussed more frequently by one party than another, or whether the word choices associated with “security” change between government and opposition speeches. This makes STM especially useful for electoral research because it connects textual patterns to political context. However, metadata associations are generally descriptive rather than automatically causal; party affiliation may correlate with other factors, such as region or campaign period.

Everyday analogy: LDA sorts recipes according to their ingredients. STM additionally records who cooked each recipe, when it was prepared, and for what occasion, then examines whether those details predict the ingredients or the way a dish is described.

Key papers: Roberts, Stewart, and Airoldi (2013); Roberts et al. (2014).[1]

Topic

23. Text Classification / Stance Detection

Text classification assigns a document or message to predefined categories. A model learns from labeled examples in which humans have already identified the correct class—for example, whether a post concerns corruption, security, or the economy. The model converts text into measurable features, such as words, phrases, or contextual representations, and learns patterns associated with each category. Stance detection is a specialized classification task that identifies an author’s position toward a target, such as supporting, opposing, or remaining neutral toward a candidate, policy, or electoral claim. The target matters: a message may be positive in tone but oppose a particular politician, or criticize one proposal while supporting another. In Mexican electoral research, stance labels should therefore specify both the target and the possible positions. Performance is commonly assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, and the F1 score, but human annotation quality and political context are equally important.

Everyday analogy: A librarian first sorts books into subjects; a stance detector goes further by marking whether a review recommends, rejects, or is undecided about a specific book.

Key papers: Matsuno and Carley (2004) on stance classification; Mohammad et al. (2016) on stance detection in social media.[5]

Topic

24. Named Entity Recognition (NER) / BIO Tags

Named Entity Recognition (NER) identifies references to people, organizations, places, dates, parties, and other predefined entity types in text. Rather than classifying an entire document, NER labels individual tokens within a sentence. For example, in “Claudia Sheinbaum visited Puebla,” the system might label “Claudia Sheinbaum” as a person and “Puebla” as a location. BIO tags specify where entities begin and end: B means “beginning,” I means “inside,” and O means “outside” an entity. Thus, “Claudia Sheinbaum visited Puebla” could be labeled B-PERSON, I-PERSON, O, B-LOCATION. BIO tagging is important because multiword names require boundaries, and the same word can have different meanings in different contexts. In electoral research, customized entity categories may be needed for candidates, coalitions, electoral institutions, municipalities, and political programs. Evaluation usually compares predicted entity spans and types with human-labeled annotations.

Everyday analogy: NER is like highlighting names and places in a newspaper, while BIO tags indicate exactly where each highlighted phrase starts and ends.

Key papers: Ramshaw and Marcus (1995) introduced IOB-style tagging; Tjong Kim Sang and De Meulder (2003) evaluated standard NER systems.

Topic

25. Conditional Random Field (CRF)

A Conditional Random Field (CRF) is a supervised probabilistic model that predicts a sequence of labels while considering relationships between neighboring labels. This is valuable for tasks such as NER, where the correct label for one word depends partly on nearby words and on the labels assigned to surrounding words. For example, an I-PERSON tag should normally follow a B-PERSON or another I-PERSON tag, not an unrelated location label. A CRF estimates which complete label sequence is most probable given the observed sentence. It uses features such as the current word, capitalization, suffixes, neighboring words, and previous predicted labels. Unlike a generative model, a CRF models the conditional probability of labels given the input text, rather than modeling how both text and labels were generated. CRFs were widely used before neural language models and remain useful when labeled data are limited or when structured, valid output sequences are important.

Everyday analogy: Instead of identifying each word independently, a proofreader checks an entire sentence: if one word begins a person’s name, the following words are more likely to continue that same name.

Key paper: Lafferty, McCallum, and Pereira (2001).

Topic

26. BETO / BERT / Masked Language Modeling (MLM)

Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) is a neural language model based on the Transformer architecture. Unlike older models that read text mainly from left to right, BERT uses attention to examine words in both directions and represent each word according to its surrounding context (Devlin et al. 2019). It is pretrained through Masked Language Modeling (MLM): some input tokens are hidden, and the model learns to predict them from the remaining words. For example, in “The candidate promised better [MASK],” the model uses context to infer a plausible missing word. After pretraining, BERT can be fine-tuned for tasks such as classification, stance detection, and NER. BETO is a Spanish-language BERT model trained on large Spanish corpora, making it more appropriate than an English-only model for many Mexican electoral texts (Cañete et al. 2020). Nevertheless, performance depends on domain, dialect, political slang, code-switching, and the quality of task-specific labels.

Everyday analogy: A student studies thousands of sentences with selected words covered by tape, learns to infer the missing words from context, and later applies that contextual understanding to identify names or political positions.

Key papers: Devlin et al. (2019); Cañete et al. (2020).
